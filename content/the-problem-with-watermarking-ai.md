title: The Problem With Watermarking AI
subtitle: Knowing that something was generated by AI is not the same as knowing whether it is trustworthy.

The EU AI Act’s Article 50 is now in force. Among other requirements, it obliges providers of generative AI systems to make AI-generated or manipulated content detectable through machine-readable marking. The European Commission’s Code of Practice turns that principle into something much more specific: for free-form text longer than 200 tokens, the recommended compliance mechanism is an imperceptible watermark.

The intention is hard to argue with. AI has made it harder to know where digital content came from. A photograph, a video, a voice recording—or an article—may have been produced by a machine rather than a person. Giving people some way to establish provenance seems useful, especially when the content is meant to inform or influence the public.

But the EU is making a category error.

Knowing that something was generated by AI is not the same as knowing whether it is trustworthy. That distinction matters because provenance is becoming easier to establish precisely as authenticity is becoming harder.

A watermark can tell me that a model probably generated a paragraph. It cannot tell me whether the paragraph is accurate. It cannot tell me whether the author used AI to produce the entire argument or merely to rewrite a sentence. It cannot tell me whether the person publishing it agrees with it. And it cannot tell me whether I should believe it.

The watermark answers the wrong question.

The deeper problem is that text is not like an image. With an image, provenance can be a meaningful property of the object itself. We can record that a photograph was captured by a particular camera, edited in Photoshop, and later processed by an AI system. The history of the file is useful information.

Text is different because writing is inherently transformative. I can ask a model to generate an essay, rewrite it myself, translate it, edit it with another model, rearrange the paragraphs, cut half of it, and publish it under my own name. At what point does the text stop being “AI-generated”?

The law has to draw a line somewhere. Technology then has to manufacture a signal capable of surviving on the other side of that line. That is where watermarking becomes uncomfortable.

The machine is no longer merely generating text. It is subtly altering the text so that someone else can later determine that the machine generated it. The alteration may be statistically tiny. It may have no noticeable effect on quality. But something has changed: the output now contains information intended for a third party, whether or not the person using the model wants it there.

That is a strange model for the future of software. More importantly, it is unlikely to solve the underlying problem.

Anyone seriously motivated to conceal AI assistance will have alternatives: paraphrase the output, pass it through another model, translate it, edit it, or use a system that does not participate in the same ecosystem. The watermark therefore risks becoming most effective against ordinary users—the people who were never particularly interested in deception in the first place.

And this reveals the larger mistake. We are trying to restore an old internet assumption: if content looks human, assume it came from a human. That assumption is disappearing, and no watermark can bring it back.

The better response is not to make every piece of generated text secretly identifiable. It is to build a world in which provenance is available when it matters, but trust is earned somewhere else. That means open standards, verifiable provenance, editorial responsibility, identifiable authorship, and reputation. It means treating digital information with something closer to zero-trust principles: do not assume that content is authentic merely because it looks authentic.

Article 50 is addressing a real problem. But watermarking should be understood as a narrow provenance mechanism, not as a solution to the authenticity crisis created by generative AI.

The internet is about to contain more synthetic content than ever before. The important question is not whether we can identify every machine-written sentence. It is whether we can build systems in which knowing where something came from is useful—without pretending that it tells us what to believe.
